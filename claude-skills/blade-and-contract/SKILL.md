# Blade and Contract

Make the claim memorable and make its meaning defensible. The blade is the arresting claim; the contract is the evidence, definition, scope, or test that supports it.

## Start with the actual request

Use the requested audience, medium, tone, and length. Infer routine details and draft without a questionnaire. A single title or sentence needs a direct edit, not a positioning workshop. Use a deeper pass when the argument or public claim warrants one. Drafting does not authorize publication.

Strong language is appropriate when requested. Precision is required regardless of tone. Follow applicable user instructions over advisory style conventions within the app's instruction hierarchy.

## Sharpen the claim

1. Find the consequence a reader should care about: a failed assumption, changed capability, measurable cost, or new possibility. State it directly.
2. Identify what establishes the claim: object, denominator, intervention, baseline, metric, protocol, or boundary. Put decisive qualifications close enough to prevent a misleading reading.
3. Test the strongest reasonable objection. If it exposes contrary evidence or a genuine limitation, revise the claim. If it uses a different definition, explain the distinction fairly.
4. Cut loose hype, ambiguous scope, invented novelty, and unnecessary combativeness. Keep force where the evidence supports it.
5. Deliver the requested artifact. Include a short evidence note only when useful or requested; do not append the internal review process by default.

Do not manufacture an opponent or force every argument into "X is not Y." A direct positive claim is often stronger. Do not design wording to make legitimate criticism harder to understand.

## Truth constraints

- Retain material uncertainty even when it weakens a slogan. Words such as "may," "preliminary," or "to our knowledge" can be necessary; remove them only when the evidence warrants removal.
- Distinguish measurements, demonstrated mechanisms, hypotheses, and projections. Do not present an operational definition as empirical proof.
- A disclaimer later in the document does not repair a misleading headline. Qualify the headline or narrow its claim.
- Preserve numerical denominators, baselines, conditions, and source attribution. Never invent a receipt, quote, priority claim, or result.
- Do not turn every limitation into a rhetorical advantage. Sometimes a limitation simply limits the claim.

## Review lens

- **Defensible force:** clear, memorable, supported. Keep it.
- **Unsupported force:** stronger than the evidence. Narrow it or obtain the missing evidence.
- **Accurate but dull:** make the consequence concrete without inflating certainty.
- **Vague grandiosity:** replace adjectives with the intervention, result, and conditions.

Example, given a measured result:

> In this benchmark, the change cut runtime from 12 minutes to 8 across 20 tasks.

An assertive version can lead with:

> One change cut benchmark runtime by a third.

Keep the task count, baseline, and benchmark scope nearby. Do not turn this into a universal speedup claim.

For a short artifact, check meaning and support once. For a substantive launch or paper, review sources and objections in proportion to the stakes. Delegate independent evidence checks only when the harness permits it and useful work can proceed concurrently; keep final voice and claim strength coherent.
